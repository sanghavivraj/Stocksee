What is a mutual fund, in plain words?
Imagine a big basket. Thousands of people put a little money in. A fund manager uses the pooled money to hold many shares or bonds. Each person owns slices of the basket, called units. You do not pick the shares yourself — you own a share of the basket.

Why does the same ₹5,000 buy different units?
Think of a pizza cut into slices. If one slice costs ₹100, ₹5,000 gets you 50 slices. If the price drops to ₹80, the same ₹5,000 gets 62.5 slices. A fixed amount always buys more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high. That is all "rupee cost averaging" means.

What is compounding?
Growth that earns its own growth. If ₹100 grows 10% you get ₹110. Next year 10% is on ₹110, not ₹100, so you get ₹121. The longer it runs, the bigger the "growth on growth" part. Drag the Years slider and watch the golden area pull away from the green dashed line.

Formula: FV = P × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i), with i = monthly rate and n = months. A steady return is an assumption — real markets are bumpy (next chapter).

Why does a 20% fall need a 25% rise to recover?
Start with ₹100. A 20% fall leaves ₹80. A 20% rise on ₹80 is only ₹96 — still short. To get back to ₹100 you need ₹20 more, and ₹20 is 25% of ₹80. The deeper the fall, the bigger the climb: 50% down needs 100% up. Try the slider above.
Is this what will happen?
No. The fall size, the recovery time and the 12% trend are all numbers we chose. Real recoveries can be quicker, slower, or not happen in your timeline. Chapter 8 shows real falls.

How do I read this chart?
Every thin line is one pretend future. Red lines ended with less than was put in. The thick lines show the unlucky (5th percentile), middle and lucky (95th) results. If the lines fan out wide, outcomes are very uncertain. Try 3 years vs 20 years, or bigger ups and downs.

How is this calculated?
Units bought = monthly amount ÷ NAV on the instalment day. If that day was a holiday or weekend, the next day that has a NAV is used. Worth = total units × the NAV on the last date. Gain/loss = worth − money put in. XIRR is the single yearly rate that matches all the dated cash flows; it is shown only for periods of a year or more. Not included: exit load, taxes, stamp duty, and any SIP charges. NAV already has the fund's running costs taken out.
Why can I not pick "IDCW" or "Payout" funds?
These funds pay money out, and the NAV drops each time. The simple units × NAV maths would then give a wrong answer, so only other plans (such as Growth) are supported.

  • A SIP is a fixed amount, on a fixed date, put in automatically.
  • Your money buys units. The same ₹5,000 buys more units when the price is low, fewer when it is high.
  • More years give growth more time to build on itself (compounding).
  • Prices fall as well as rise. A fall of 20% needs a 25% rise to get back; real history in Chapter 8 shows how long real falls took.
  • A fall becomes a fixed loss only if the units are sold at the lower price.
  • Past returns are history, not a promise. Any future rate in a story is an assumption.
